Why governance is the first thing you design, not the last

Most platform failures inside multi agency systems are governance failures wearing a technical disguise. The symptoms look like performance problems, data conflicts, or integration bugs—but the root cause is almost always an unresolved question about who has authority over what, and who decided that.

I learned this building the UN Migration Network Hub, a Drupal 10 platform serving 195 member states and 64 UN agencies. The platform doesn't just host content; it encodes the diplomatic contracts between organizations that fundamentally disagree on data ownership, editorial workflows, and visibility rules. Every content type, every permission, every entity reference is a governance decision rendered in code.

The governance layer is the architecture

When a platform serves multiple sovereign organizations, the architecture isn't the database schema or the API layer—it's the permission model. The technical implementation merely enforces what the governance layer defines. If you design the schema first and figure out permissions later, you'll rebuild the schema. Every time.

The UN Migration Network Hub has three organizational tiers, each with distinct authority boundaries: IOM headquarters (platform steward), 64 UN agencies (content authorities for their domains), and 195 member states (consumers and occasional contributors). The platform doesn't impose uniformity—it provides the primitives for each tier to define its own rules while maintaining cross-referential integrity.

The CMS conversation asks what software renders your pages. The real question is what system enforces who is allowed to say what, to whom, and that question has nothing to do with themes.

Where the skepticism is fair

None of this means Drupal is the right tool for everything. If your product is a fast moving consumer app with a small, trusted team and no governance complexity, a lighter framework will move faster and cost less to run. The skepticism about Drupal is earned when it is used as a website builder for a problem that never needed one. It stops being earned the moment your real problem is structured data with real ownership rules attached to it, because that is exactly the problem Drupal was built to solve.

Entities, not pages

The part of Drupal that does the real work in institutional platforms is the entity system: structured content types with fields, relationships, and revisions, exposed consistently through an API layer whether you are building a public statistics portal or an internal governance tool. Once you stop thinking in pages and start thinking in entities, Drupal stops being a CMS and starts being what it actually is—a framework for building data driven applications on top of a mature, security audited core.

That framing matters when you are choosing a stack for an institution rather than a product. A startup can rewrite its stack in eighteen months if the bet does not pay off. A UN agency cannot. The platforms I build need to survive staff turnover, budget cycles, and a decade or more of production life. Drupal's content model, its permission system, and its enormous surface area of vetted contributed modules exist precisely because thousands of other institutions have needed the same governance primitives before.
